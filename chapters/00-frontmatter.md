## Preface

I wrote this book because the cost of producing a finance deliverable collapsed,
and almost nobody noticed what that did to the cost of trusting one. A model will
now write you a clean variance note, a tidy reconciliation summary, a confident
liquidity paragraph — in seconds, in the right vocabulary, formatted for the deck.
The output looks finished. The hard part — knowing whether it is *true*, whether
it ties to a source, whether someone can stand behind it in front of a controller
or an audit committee — got no cheaper at all. If anything, it got harder, because
fluent output is so much better now at impersonating finished work. This book is
about the gap between those two things, and about building finance pipelines that
respect it instead of papering over it.

Why now: because the failure mode has already arrived in real close cycles. Junior
analysts paste model output into decks without checking the export. Reviewers
approve language that *sounds* right without asking what it is grounded in. The
discipline that finance has always depended on — source checks, control totals,
threshold reviews, owner sign-offs, release gates — is exactly the discipline that
generated text is built to skip. I think the answer is not to ban the tools or to
trust them, but to wire the friction back in deliberately: to treat the machine as
an excellent drafter and the human as the accountable judge, and to make the
boundary between them a thing you can test.

Why me: I teach artificial intelligence, data science, and statistics at
Northeastern University, and for the past several years I have been building
agentic, verified, CLI-agnostic book-repositories — manuscripts that are also
working systems, governed by a constitution that asks machines to verify
conformance and humans to verify adequacy. Mycroft is one of them. Through
Humanitarians AI, the nonprofit I founded, I work with graduates learning to build
production-scale AI with public evidence of real work, and the lesson that keeps
recurring is the one this book is built on: the artifact is never the evidence of
judgment. The process, the friction, and the willingness to be wrong out loud are.

A word on what this book does not do. It is not a course in accounting, GAAP, or
audit standards — it assumes you bring that judgment and shows you where to apply
it. It is not a manual for the Snickerdoodle framework that governs the
repository; that lives in `SNICKERDOODLE.md`. It does not automate journal
posting, payment release, filings, or investor communication, and it will argue
forcefully that you should not either. It is a book about building verified finance
recipes — and about the analyst, still indispensable, who decides what the numbers
mean.

— Nik Bear Brown
